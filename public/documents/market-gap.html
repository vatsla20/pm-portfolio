The Gap Between Building a Feature and Building a Product

My Journey Building an EdTech Product During COVID-19

I built Nova for the crisis. I didn't ask: what happens when the crisis ends?

That question has shaped how I think about product strategy ever since.

Context: The Problem in 2020

In March 2020, schools shut overnight. Millions of household, particularly those with limited parental literacy had no fallback for early childhood education. As a software developer, I saw a problem I couldn't ignore.

Nova was an educational mobile app for toddlers (ages 1-5). It solved a real problem in a real moment. And then the moment passed.

Here's what I learned from that experience.

What Made Nova Different

Nova wasn't another learning platform. That distinction matters.

Platforms like YouTube Kids or Khan Academy assume a child can sit, watch, follow, and comprehend. That model works for older children. It does nothing for a 2-year-old.

The Core Insight: Interaction IS Learning

For toddlers, attention spans are measured in seconds. Learning doesn't happen by watching. It happens through touch, sound, repetition, and play.

Instead of passive videos, Nova gave toddlers something to interact with:

  • Tap an animal → hear it roar
  • Trace a letter with your finger
  • Match sounds to faces
  • Sing along to rhymes with moving visuals
The interaction was the learning, not a means to it.

Designed for Low-Literacy Households

Crucially, Nova was built for households where parents had limited education themselves. The child could pick it up, play, and learn independently with no parental guidance required.

That was the product. That was the gap it filled.

The Hard Question: Is Nova Still Relevant?

Honestly? The original 2020 version would struggle today.

Why the market shifted:

  • AI-native kids apps are everywhere
  • Schools reopened; traditional teaching evolved
  • The urgency of 2020 is gone
  • The low bar that let scrappy solutions thrive no longer exists

But the Problem Evolved, It Didn't Disappear

The children Nova served in 2020 are now 6-10 years old. They're back in school, but the parents who couldn't teach a toddler still can't help with homework.

Low-literacy households still exist. The homework struggle is real, daily, and almost entirely unaddressed. Parents are improvising with YouTube, Google, and AI tools built for adults—then handing them to children.

The gap Nova was built for is still there. It just changed shape.

What I'd Build Today: Nova 2.0

If I were building Nova in 2026, I'd start with the same insight—the interaction is the learning—but build a fundamentally different product.

Core Strategy: Product That Grows With the Child

Original Nova: Solved for one moment (toddler learning during lockdown)

Nova 2.0: Solves for a trajectory

  • Toddler foundations at age 2
  • Homework support by age 7
  • Additional curriculum as they progress through school
  • One product, one familiar experience, across 10 years

Key Features: How Nova 2.0 Would Work

1. AI-Personalized Learning

The Problem: No two toddlers learn at the same pace. One picks up alphabets in days; another needs weeks on animal sounds. The original Nova treated every child with the same sequence, speed, and content.

The Solution: AI that watches how a child interacts, follows where they hesitate, what they skip, what makes them tap again and quietly reshapes the experience around that specific child's pace.

Not a fixed curriculum. A living one that moves with the learner.

2. Teaching the Parent, Not Just the Child

This is the piece I missed entirely. And it might be the most important one.

In low-literacy households, the problem isn't only that the child needs help.It's that the parent doesn't know how to help. They feel shut out of their child's learning, so they hand over the phone and step away.

Nova 2.0 changes that with an AI layer that works alongside the parent:

  • Shows what their child is learning this week (plain, simple language)
  • Gives one small thing to try: "Ask your child what sound a lion makes tonight"
  • Explains, without jargon, why certain activities matter at this developmental stage
  • Builds parental confidence to participate in learning, not just observe
The goal isn't to turn a parent into a teacher overnight. It's to close the distance between parent and child, one small prompt at a time.

3. Parental Dashboard

A simple, visual dashboard that answers the one question every parent has: Is my child doing okay?

Displays:

  • What they practiced this week
  • Where they're struggling
  • Time spent on activities
  • Gentle nudge when app hasn't been opened in days

Designed for low-literacy parents:

  • Heavy on visuals, light on text
  • No jargon, no data overload
  • A dashboard is useless if the parent can't navigate it

4. Vernacular Language Support

A child learns best in the language spoken at home. For most of India, that isn't English.

Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi the content, voice prompts, nursery rhymes. Everything must exist in the language a child actually hears their mother speak.

This isn't a nice-to-have feature for a later sprint.

For Nova's target audience, it's the difference between the app feeling familiar vs. foreign. One gets used. The other gets deleted.

5. Offline-First Architecture

The households Nova serves often have the weakest internet connections. That hasn't changed.

Connectivity cannot be a prerequisite for learning.

Every core feature like games, tracing, audio, rhymes, homework support, etc all must work completely offline. Sync when there's a signal. Learn regardless of conditions.

The Real Lesson: Features vs. Products

I built Nova as a developer. I thought in features, not users. In screens, not systems. I solved the problem in front of me and didn't ask where that problem was going.

Products That Last Are Built for Trajectories

  • Not for moments, but for the user as they are today and as they'll be tomorrow
  • COVID gave Nova urgency, but urgency isn't a product strategy
  • The same insight (interaction is learning) could have powered a 10-year product. I just didn't see it in 2020

What This Taught Me About Product Management

  1. Market timing ≠ Product-market fit — solving an urgent problem doesn't mean you've built a sustainable product

  2. User needs evolve, but the underlying problem often persists — the children who needed Nova at 2 still need support at 7; I just wasn't thinking that far ahead

  3. Product strategy requires asking "what happens next?" — not just "does this solve today's problem?"

  4. The best products grow with their users — one-time solutions get replaced; products that adapt become habits

  5. Involving stakeholders (parents) I initially excluded would have made the product stickier — I optimized for independence when I should have optimized for engagement

If I Were Building Nova Today

Same insight: The interaction is the learning

Different execution:

  • Product that grows with the child (ages 1-10)
  • AI-personalized learning paths
  • Parent-inclusive design (teaching parents, not replacing them)
  • Vernacular language support from day one
  • Offline-first architecture
  • Dashboard that builds parental confidence
Same problem. Sharper product.

Reflection

Nova taught me the difference between building something that works and building something that lasts. I built for a crisis. I should have built for a trajectory.

That's the gap between a feature and a product.
